# Day 11 — Numeral: checkable budgets

## Concept

"Give me some options for {{TASK}}" has no numeral. "Some" could be two or twenty; nothing tells the model when to stop generating. Length is just as unbounded — an "option" could be a phrase or a paragraph.

A numeral turns a fuzzy amount into a number you can count against. "Exactly three options ... each under 40 words, ranked by cost" bounds several dimensions at once: how many, how long each is, and, by "ranked", that there's an order to check. Each is verifiable by counting, not judging.

Vague quantifiers — "a few", "several", "some" — feel like constraints but aren't: two readers would satisfy them differently and both be right. A number removes that disagreement; three is three, whoever counts.

The dimensions needing a number rarely stop at one. Count, length, and any other countable property each need their own bound, or the one left open becomes the loophole the constraint leaks through.

Here is the test: for each quantity in your prompt, ask whether you could check it with a count, not a feeling. If the answer is "a few" or "several", replace it.

## Before / After

**Before**

> Give me some options for {{TASK}}.

**After**

> Give me exactly three options for {{TASK}}, each under 40 words, ranked by cost.

"Some" became "exactly three"; the open-ended "options" got a length bound too. Every dimension a reader could have miscounted is now a number you can check with a count.

## Exercise

Pick one of your recurring tasks. Write a single prompt for it.

### Novice

Fill the blanks with a count and a length, then send the completed line as your prompt.

> Give me exactly ________ options for {{TASK}}, each under ________ words.

### Working

Write a prompt for {{TASK}} that bounds every countable dimension — count, length, and any other quantity — with a number instead of a vague quantifier.

### Advanced

Write a prompt for {{TASK}} where every constraint is verifiable without judgement — a reader could check each one with a count, not an opinion.
