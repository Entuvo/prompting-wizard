# Day 12 — Interjection: attention markers

## Concept

Five instructions in a row read as five equal priorities, even when one matters far more than the rest. A must-not-fail instruction sitting third from last, unmarked, between a style note and a summary request, carries no more weight than either neighbour. Working through in order, a model can satisfy four well and quietly drop the fifth — it looked least important, because nothing said otherwise.

An interjection like "IMPORTANT:" breaks that equality, telling the reader — human or model — that this instruction isn't like its neighbours. But marking alone isn't enough: buried mid-paragraph, it still competes for attention. Position matters as much as the word. Standing alone, the same sentence becomes the hardest thing to have missed.

The marker is only honest if rare. If everything is IMPORTANT, nothing is. One marker, on the instruction whose failure you'd actually be angry about, makes the word mean something.

Here is the test: find the sentence you'd be angriest to see ignored. Is it marked, and could a skimming reader still miss it? If yes, move it or mark it until the answer is no.
