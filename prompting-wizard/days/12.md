# Day 12 — Interjection: attention markers

## Concept

Five instructions in a row read as five equal priorities, even when one matters far more than the rest. A must-not-fail instruction sitting second from last, unmarked, between a style note and a summary request, carries no more weight than either neighbour. Working through in order, a model can satisfy four well and quietly drop the fifth — it looked least important, because nothing said otherwise.

An interjection like "IMPORTANT:" breaks that equality, telling the reader — human or model — that this instruction isn't like its neighbours. But marking alone isn't enough: buried mid-paragraph, it still competes for attention. Standing alone, the same sentence becomes the hardest thing to have missed.

The marker is only honest if rare. If everything is IMPORTANT, nothing is. Marked inline among its neighbours is where this starts; standing alone but competing with one other marked item is the climb from there; exactly one marker, on the instruction whose failure you'd actually be angry about, is what makes the word mean something.

Here is the test: count the markers in your prompt, and ask whether the one you'd be angriest to see ignored stands alone on its own line.

## Before / After

This fixed example illustrates the technique; apply it to your own task in the exercise.

**Before**

> Review the offer letter for clarity and tone. Note repeated information. Suggest clearer headings where useful. Do not suggest changing the quoted prices. Summarise your findings in up to five bullets; use fewer if there are fewer findings.

**After**

> Review the offer letter for clarity and tone. Note repeated information. Suggest clearer headings where useful. Summarise your findings in up to five bullets; use fewer if there are fewer findings.
>
> IMPORTANT: Do not suggest changing the quoted prices.

The instruction didn't change; its weight did. Buried in the paragraph and unmarked, it read as one of five equal notes. Marked and moved to stand alone at the end, it reads as the one thing that cannot slip.

## Exercise

Pick one of your recurring tasks and its most important constraint: an offer letter with fixed prices, a school notice with a fixed date, or a research summary that must preserve quoted wording. Write a single prompt for it.

### Novice

Write a five-sentence prompt for {{TASK}} as a single paragraph, containing one instruction you'd be angriest to see ignored. Mark that instruction IMPORTANT: where it sits — inline in the paragraph, not moved and not on a line of its own — then send the completed prompt.

### Working

Write a prompt for {{TASK}} with two marked instructions — the one that must not fail, standing alone on its own line, and one other you also marked. Say which of the two is the one that must not fail.

### Advanced

Write a prompt for {{TASK}} with exactly one attention marker, on the instruction you'd be angriest to see ignored, standing alone as its own line. Every other instruction carries no marker.
