# Day 8 — Pronoun: reference binding

## Concept

"Look at this and fix it." Without named material, "this" could mean an invitation, a schedule, or a whole event plan; "it" could mean the document or one incorrect detail. The model has to guess which thing you want changed.

A pronoun is a shortcut for a noun already named — it only works once naming happens first. "Read the notice below. It gives the wrong venue. Replace that venue with Cedar Hall" keeps the references close to what they mean: "It" points to "the notice below", and "that venue" names the detail to replace.

Ambiguity survives even when an antecedent exists: if two nouns are candidates and either reading works, the pronoun is still broken. The reader — human or model — picks one silently, and you never find out it picked wrong.

Here is the test: for each it/this/that/these in your prompt, point at the exact word or quoted block it refers to. One you can't point at, with the rest resolving, is partway. Replacing that last one is the climb; putting each antecedent next to its pronoun is the rung above.

## Before / After

This fixed example illustrates resolving references by naming the intended object. It is not the advanced exercise, which keeps pronouns and places their antecedents nearby. Apply the technique to your own task in the exercise.

**Before**

> Read this. It gives the wrong venue. Replace it with Cedar Hall.
>
> Notice: Volunteer welcome session, Friday at 10 am, Oak Room.

**After**

> Read the notice below. It gives the wrong venue. Replace that venue with Cedar Hall.
>
> Notice: Volunteer welcome session, Friday at 10 am, Oak Room.

The first sentence now names the notice, so "It" has a clear reference. "Replace that venue" specifies which detail changes; the second "it" in the original could leave the reader wondering whether to replace the venue or rewrite the notice. The requested correction stays the same.

## Exercise

Pick one of your recurring tasks involving more than one document or detail: updating an invitation, comparing two reports, or revising a classroom handout. Write a single prompt for it.

### Novice

Take a prompt for {{TASK}} that uses at least two pronouns with no antecedent, then rewrite it by replacing all but one of them with the exact noun phrase each should have meant — leaving exactly one pronoun that still requires the reader to guess — and send the rewritten prompt.

### Working

Write a prompt for {{TASK}} where every pronoun has exactly one possible antecedent inside the prompt or in a block you quote, even if the reader has to look back a sentence or two to find it.

### Advanced

Write a prompt for {{TASK}} where each pronoun's antecedent is the nearest preceding noun phrase, so no reader has to look back past the previous clause. Keep the pronouns — replacing them with nouns is not the exercise.
